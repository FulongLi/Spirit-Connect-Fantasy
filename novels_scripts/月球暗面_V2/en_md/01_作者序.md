# 01 Author's Note
People often say that what we think by day becomes what we dream by night. But what if dreams are not the afterimage of thought, but the activity of consciousness in another dimension?
I have always suspected that the state we call "reality" is only one of consciousness's many working modes. Dreams, perhaps, are merely the natural form consciousness takes after briefly leaving the constraints of reality.
Have you ever had a dream like this?
It has no relation to ordinary experience, yet it possesses an almost perfect logic and detail. It is strange, vast, even more stable than waking memory. After you wake, you cannot explain where it came from, yet you are certain: it was not fantasy.
Do you still remember when you had your first dream? Or was that truly the "first"?
Physiologically, everyone dreams. But in subjective experience, dreams are not the same as other dreams. Some are like noise, random, broken, quickly dispersed. Others are like fragments that have been fully recorded, with continuous space, a clear timeline, even a self-consistent history. Some people spend their whole lives barely remembering their dreams. Others live more clearly inside dreams than they do while awake.
I happen to belong to the latter kind.
When I was young, I experienced several dreams of an abnormally vast scale. They were not blurred, nor did they leap about. They were stable, sustained, and even obeyed some kind of inner law. That experience was less like dreaming than like "remembering."
Every time I woke from one of those dreams, I would be followed by a long headache and daze, as if consciousness had just completed a crossing migration and been forcibly dragged back into the body and time point of the present. Those dreams had no direct relation to the real world. Some pointed toward ancient slices of history. Some described structures of a future that had not yet arrived. Within them were the rise and fall of civilizations, the collapse of order, and systems that ran with high precision and cold indifference. Some were full of violence and turmoil. Others were quiet, restrained, almost perfect. They did not seem like results randomly spliced together by the brain. They seemed more like the "reading" of some already existing information.
As I grew older, dreams of that kind gradually disappeared. In their place came scattered, brief, incoherent fragments, as if some channel were slowly closing. Memory, too, was decaying irreversibly.
For that reason, I began trying to record those dreams. Not merely to tell stories, but to describe a universe that might exist.
I arranged these records into a series and named it Spirit Connect. They appear independent of one another, yet in a deeper structure they are interconnected, like different interfaces of the same system, or multiple projections of the same truth. As for whether these contents are dreams, fantasies, or real experiences of consciousness on some unknown level, I cannot give a conclusion.
Everything described in this book is fictional. Any resemblance to actual persons or events is purely coincidental.
At least, within the present system of cognition, that is so.
-- Li Fulong
